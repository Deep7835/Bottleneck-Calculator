How to plan a balanced gaming PC build

A good PC build isn't the one with the most expensive parts. It's the one where the processor and graphics card are matched to each other and to the monitor, so nothing you paid for sits idle. This guide shows how to plan that balance, with CPU and GPU pairings for 1080p, 1440p and 4K calculated with the same model as our bottleneck calculator.

Plan in this order

For a gaming PC, plan from the screen backwards:

  1. Choose the monitor's resolution and refresh rate. This sets how hard the graphics card has to work and how many frames per second you're aiming for.
  2. Choose the graphics card for that resolution. It sets the frame rate in most games, so it gets the biggest share of the money.
  3. Choose the cheapest CPU that keeps that card busy. At your resolution, the processor should be able to prepare at least as many frames as the card can draw. Check the pair in the calculator: GPU-limited, or under 10% CPU-limited, is the goal.
  4. Adjust for the games you play. Competitive shooters at high refresh rates, simulators, strategy games and MMOs need more CPU. Cinematic single-player games need more GPU.
  5. Fill in memory, storage and power. These rarely set the frame rate, but getting them wrong causes stutter, long loads or crashes.

The reason this order works is simple. The CPU sets a frame-rate ceiling that stays the same at any resolution, while the graphics card's ceiling drops as the resolution rises. So the higher your resolution, the less CPU you need for a given card. Our guide to how resolution affects bottlenecks shows the effect in detail, and CPU vs GPU bottleneck explains what each part actually does.

Every pairing is GPU-limited or within a 10% CPU bottleneck at native resolution, which is what balanced means in practice. Three things stand out:

  • The refresh rate is a ceiling, not a requirement. Apart from the RTX 5090 at 4K, none of these builds reaches its monitor's refresh rate in demanding AAA games at native resolution, and that's normal. With variable refresh (G-Sync or FreeSync), 90 to 120 FPS feels smooth on a high-refresh monitor. The extra refresh rate pays off in competitive games, where every build here runs well above its target.
  • Upscaling shifts the balance toward the CPU. DLSS and FSR make the graphics card faster, so the processor has to keep up with a higher frame rate. At 1440p the Ryzen 5 7600 + RTX 5070 goes from GPU-limited to balanced at about 127 FPS with DLSS Quality, and the Ryzen 7 7800X3D + RTX 5070 Ti reaches about 160 FPS. If you'll always use upscaling, plan the CPU one step higher.
  • At 4K, the GPU is everything. The RTX 5080 reaches about 119 FPS at 4K with DLSS Quality. Only the RTX 5090 clears 120 FPS, at about 164.

Balanced

At 1440p the balanced pairs run roughly diagonally. A Ryzen 5 5600 or even a Core i3-12100F keeps up with an RTX 5070 in AAA games, a Ryzen 5 7600 matches an RTX 5070 Ti or RTX 5080, and only the Ryzen 7 9800X3D is balanced with an RTX 5090 (the Ryzen 7 7800X3D is 12% CPU-limited, a minor bottleneck). Go above the diagonal and you lose performance: a Ryzen 5 5600 with an RTX 5080 is 25% CPU-limited. Go below it and you've spent more on the processor than the card can use.

At 1080p, everything shifts one card down. The balanced pairs become the Ryzen 5 7600 with an RTX 5070, the Ryzen 7 7800X3D with an RTX 5070 Ti and the Ryzen 7 9800X3D with an RTX 5080. The Ryzen 5 7600 with an RTX 5080 would be 33% CPU-limited at 1080p. At 4K, the shift goes the other way and almost any modern six-core keeps up with anything short of a 5090.

How to split the budget

Rules of thumb, not laws; a good sale can change the maths.

  • Graphics card: 35 to 50% of the total, rising with resolution. For a 4K build it can be more than half.
  • CPU: 15 to 20%. For 1440p and 4K gaming, the graphics card should usually cost two to three times as much as the processor.
  • Motherboard, memory, storage, power supply and case: the rest. Count the platform, not just the CPU. AM4 and DDR4 Intel boards with DDR4 memory cost less than AM5 or DDR5, and that saving can move you up a graphics tier.

Here's why the GPU gets the bigger share. At 1440p, a Ryzen 5 7600 + RTX 5070 Ti averages about 118 FPS in AAA games, while a Ryzen 7 7800X3D + RTX 5070, which typically costs about the same, manages 101. The bigger card wins by 17%.

The exception is games that lean on the processor. In esports at 1080p the 7800X3D build is 20% faster (422 vs 353 FPS), and in simulators and strategy games at 1440p it's 28% faster. If those are your main games, shift money toward the CPU, and ideally toward an X3D chip.

RAM, storage and power supply basics

Memory

32 GB in two sticks is the right amount for a new gaming PC in 2026, and 16 GB is the minimum. Two sticks matter: a single stick runs in single-channel mode and can cost a lot of CPU performance. On AM5, DDR5-6000 CL30 is the usual sweet spot. On AM4 and DDR4 Intel boards, DDR4-3200 or 3600 is fine. Turn on EXPO or XMP in the BIOS, or the memory runs at a slow default speed. Too little memory shows up as stutter rather than lower averages: in our model, cutting a Ryzen 5 7600 and RTX 5070 build from 32 GB to 8 GB drops the 1% lows at 1440p from 78 to 65 FPS.

Storage

Use an NVMe SSD for Windows and games. 1 TB is the practical minimum, since many big releases take 100 GB or more, and 2 TB is comfortable. A good PCIe 4.0 drive is plenty for gaming; PCIe 5.0 drives cost more and load games barely any faster. Hard drives are fine for media and backups, not for games.

Power supply

Size the power supply for the graphics card, since it's the biggest consumer. NVIDIA's recommended system power is 550 W for the RTX 5060, 650 W for the RTX 5070, 750 W for the RTX 5070 Ti, 850 W for the RTX 5080 and 1000 W for the RTX 5090, and AMD publishes similar figures for its cards. Buy from a reputable brand, prefer an ATX 3.1 unit with a native 12V-2x6 cable for high-end NVIDIA cards, and add some headroom if you might upgrade later.

Motherboard, cooling and case

A mid-range B-series board (B650, B850, B760 or B550) is enough for gaming. Pricier boards add connectivity, not frame rate. Check that the BIOS supports your CPU. Make sure the case has decent front airflow, and that the CPU cooler suits the chip: boxed coolers are fine for 65 W six-cores, but X3D chips and K-series Intel parts need an aftermarket cooler.

Common pairing mistakes

A flagship CPU with a budget graphics card. A Ryzen 7 9800X3D with an RTX 5060 gets about 90 FPS at 1080p in AAA games, practically the same as a Ryzen 5 7600 and only 4 FPS more than a Core i3-12100F. The card is the limit, and the extra CPU money would have bought a much faster card.

A big graphics card on an old CPU. Dropping an RTX 5080 into a PC with a Ryzen 5 3600 leaves it 43% CPU-limited at 1440p, about 80 FPS. A Ryzen 5 7600 + RTX 5070 gets about 100 FPS for similar money. If your CPU is a few generations old, budget for both parts, or upgrade the CPU first. Our guide to fixing a CPU bottleneck covers the cheaper options.

Judging balance at the wrong resolution. A Ryzen 5 7600 with an RTX 5090 looks like a mistake at 1440p, where it's 32% CPU-limited. At 4K the same pair is 11% GPU-limited. Always check a build at the resolution you'll actually play.

An 8 GB card for a 1440p monitor. At 1440p High, modern games want about 9.5 GB of video memory. Cards like the RTX 5060 cope with upscaling and lower textures, but for 1440p a 12 or 16 GB card is the safer buy.

Spending on "future-proofing" the CPU. Most people replace the graphics card more often than the processor, so it pays to choose a platform with an upgrade path (AM5 today) rather than the fastest CPU you can afford. A mid-range chip on a modern socket can be swapped for an X3D part later.

Skimping where it doesn't show. One stick of RAM, EXPO or XMP left off, a no-name power supply or a case with no airflow all cost performance or reliability.

Frequently asked questions

What is a balanced CPU and GPU combo for 1440p?

A Ryzen 5 7600 with an RTX 5070 or 5070 Ti, or a Ryzen 7 7800X3D with an RTX 5070 Ti or 5080. The 7800X3D and 5070 Ti average about 123 FPS at 1440p High in AAA games and about 160 with DLSS Quality.

How much of a PC build budget should go to the graphics card?

For a gaming PC, roughly 35 to 50% of the total, with more at higher resolutions. The CPU usually takes 15 to 20%. For 1440p and 4K, the graphics card should cost about two to three times as much as the processor.

Is a bottleneck always bad?

No. One part always limits the frame rate. In a gaming PC you want it to be the graphics card, so a GPU-limited result is the healthy state. A CPU bottleneck under about 10% is also fine. It becomes a problem when the processor holds a much faster card back by 20% or more.

Should I buy a better CPU or a better GPU?

For AAA games at 1440p or 4K, the better GPU. At 1440p a Ryzen 5 7600 with an RTX 5070 Ti is about 17% faster than a Ryzen 7 7800X3D with an RTX 5070. For esports at 1080p or for simulators and strategy games, the faster CPU usually wins.

Do I need 32 GB of RAM for a gaming PC?

16 GB still works, but 32 GB in two sticks is the better choice for a new build in 2026. Newer games, plus a browser, Discord and launchers in the background, can push past 16 GB, and the result is stutter rather than a lower average frame rate.

What power supply do I need for a gaming PC?

Size it for the graphics card. NVIDIA recommends 550 W for an RTX 5060, 650 W for an RTX 5070, 750 W for an RTX 5070 Ti, 850 W for an RTX 5080 and 1000 W for an RTX 5090. Choose a reputable brand and leave headroom for upgrades.
